Wholesalers and distributors manage more than quantities. They manage supplier deliveries, customer orders, warehouses, sales units, pricing, returns, and movement between locations. A clear inventory system helps the business decide what can be promised and what needs to be purchased.

## Track the units customers actually buy

A product may arrive by carton, be stored by pack, and be sold by piece. The system should define the relationship between units and make it clear how a sale changes the underlying quantity. This is one reason unit-aware inventory is more reliable than a single free-text stock field.

## Connect purchasing to demand

Purchasing should use current stock, open orders, supplier lead time, and recent movement. Buying more of a slow item while a fast item is unavailable ties up cash and reduces service quality.

## Make warehouse movement visible

Record receiving, picking, dispatch, returns, and transfers as distinct steps. If a product is in transit, it should not appear as available at the destination until the destination confirms receipt. Clear status prevents double-promising stock.

## Review the right reports

Useful distribution reports include stock by warehouse, fast and slow movers, stock ageing, outstanding purchase orders, fulfilment gaps, returns, and adjustments. The purpose is to make the next decision easier: buy, transfer, count, investigate, or stop purchasing.

## Connect the order promise to available stock

Before promising a customer a quantity, distinguish available stock from reserved stock, damaged stock, and stock in transit. A warehouse may show 100 units physically present but only 70 available after existing orders are considered. This distinction protects fulfilment and customer trust.

## Build a practical warehouse rhythm

Start the day by reviewing open orders, late purchase orders, and items below their reorder level. During receiving, record what arrived before making it available. During picking, confirm the product and quantity. During dispatch, record what left and where it went. At the end of the day, investigate exceptions while the people and documents are still available.

## Handle returns as a decision

A returned product is not automatically saleable. Record the return, inspect the item, and classify it as available, damaged, quarantined, or awaiting review. This keeps the stock number connected to the product’s actual condition.

## Keep pricing and units understandable

Wholesale businesses often sell by carton, case, pack, and piece. Document conversions and make the selling unit visible to staff. Also record the price basis clearly so a quantity conversion does not silently create an incorrect margin.

## A distribution implementation checklist

Before go-live, confirm the product catalogue, supplier list, customer list, warehouse locations, units, opening balances, and user roles. Test one complete flow from purchase to receiving, storage, customer order, picking, dispatch, invoice, return, and stock reconciliation.

Pay special attention to partial deliveries and backorders. A customer order should show what is ready now, what is reserved, and what is waiting for a supplier. This makes customer communication more honest and helps the purchasing team prioritise the next order.

## Questions for a vendor

Ask whether the system can show stock by warehouse, support transfers, record stock in transit, separate available from reserved quantities, and preserve an audit trail. Ask how the platform handles a product sold in different units and whether reports can be filtered by location, supplier, customer, or product category.

The right system should shorten the distance between a customer promise and the stock record that supports it.
