Excel is flexible, familiar, and inexpensive. That makes it a sensible starting point for some businesses. The question is not whether Excel is bad; it is whether the spreadsheet still gives the team a trustworthy operational picture.

## When a spreadsheet may be enough

A spreadsheet may work when one person manages a small product list, there is one location, stock changes are infrequent, and every movement can be reconciled manually. Even then, backups, permissions, and clear ownership matter.

## When a spreadsheet becomes a risk

The risk grows when several people edit the record, sales happen at a till, products have multiple units, stock moves between branches, or managers need a live view. A spreadsheet can record a number, but it does not naturally enforce the workflow that explains how the number changed.

Typical symptoms include duplicated files, overwritten formulas, delayed updates, unapproved adjustments, and long stock-count exercises. These issues make it harder to know whether an apparent profit is real.

## What software adds

Inventory management software connects actions to stock. A purchase receipt can increase available stock, a sale can reduce it, a transfer can move it between locations, and an adjustment can retain a reason and user history. Reporting can then highlight the exceptions worth investigating.

The move should be planned. Clean product names, standard units, supplier information, opening balances, and a clear counting process before migration. Do not import duplicate or unverified records simply because they exist in the spreadsheet.

## The hidden cost of staying manual

The cost of a spreadsheet is not only the licence or the staff member who maintains it. Consider the time spent reconciling duplicate files, the margin lost when a product is bought urgently, the sale missed because a branch could not see stock elsewhere, and the management time spent investigating unexplained differences.

These costs are easy to miss because they appear in different parts of the business. A useful comparison should include the value of cash tied up in excess stock, the cost of stockouts, the time required for counts, and the risk of unauthorised changes.

## A safer migration path

1. Freeze the product list and remove duplicates.
2. Define units, locations, suppliers, and opening balances.
3. Count a representative sample and resolve differences.
4. Import clean data into a test workspace.
5. Run a pilot with receiving, selling, returns, and adjustments.
6. Compare the new records with the old process before expanding.

Keep the original spreadsheet as a read-only archive during the transition. Do not ask staff to maintain two live systems indefinitely; that creates two competing versions of the truth.

## When should a business move?

Move when the current process is already slowing decisions, not only when the business reaches a particular product count. A small team with valuable stock and several people handling it may need control earlier than a larger business with a very simple operation.

## What a good replacement should feel like

Staff should not need to understand the database to complete a normal task. Receiving should guide them through what arrived. Selling should make the correct product and unit clear. A transfer should show where stock came from and where it is going. A manager should be able to investigate a difference without asking five people for separate files.

The system should also make the exceptional cases visible. If a delivery is short, a product is returned damaged, or a quantity is adjusted, the reason should be recorded close to the event. This creates better information for later reporting.

## Questions to ask during a trial

- Can two users work at the same time without overwriting one another?
- Can managers restrict adjustments and review a history of changes?
- Can the system show what is available by location?
- Can the business export its own catalogue and transactions?
- Can staff complete the core POS process when connectivity drops?
