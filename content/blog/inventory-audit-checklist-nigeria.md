An inventory audit compares the physical operation with the records used to make purchasing and selling decisions. It is not only a count of products. A useful audit asks why quantities differ and whether the business can trust the process that produced them.

## Before the count

Define the locations, products, units, and time of the audit. Decide how sales, receiving, transfers, and returns will be handled while counting. Prepare a list of products and assign teams, but where appropriate use a blind count so staff record what they see rather than copying the expected balance.

## Physical count checklist

- Confirm the product identity and unit.
- Separate saleable, damaged, returned, and quarantined stock.
- Record opened cartons and partial packs consistently.
- Mark counted areas to prevent a second count.
- Record differences without silently correcting them.
- Review high-value and fast-moving items separately.

## Transaction review

Compare the count with recent receiving, sales, transfers, returns, and adjustments. Look for deliveries that were recorded late, products sold in a different unit, transfers that were dispatched but not received, and returns that were placed back into stock without inspection.

## People and permissions

Review who can create products, approve purchases, receive goods, adjust quantities, process returns, and change prices. The purpose is not to assume wrongdoing. It is to ensure that important actions have clear ownership and that exceptions can be investigated.

## Turn findings into improvements

Classify every difference: data error, receiving error, unit conversion, damage, loss, timing issue, or unexplained variance. Fix the root process and record the approved adjustment. Recount a sample after corrections to confirm that the solution worked.

## Suggested audit rhythm

Use daily exception reviews, weekly cycle counts for priority products, monthly location reviews, and a deeper periodic audit based on the business risk. A smaller repeatable process is usually more useful than a large count that happens only after a serious discrepancy.

## What owners often discover during an audit

An audit may reveal that stock is not disappearing; it is being recorded under another unit, location, product name, or transaction status. It may show that a supplier delivery was accepted before it was fully counted, that a branch transfer was never confirmed, or that returns went back to a shelf without inspection. These findings are process signals.

The audit should also look at cash-related behaviour. Compare discounts, voids, returns, price changes, and stock adjustments with the permission and approval rules. The purpose is not to accuse staff. It is to make high-impact actions visible and give managers a fair way to investigate exceptions.

## A short audit report template

Record the audit date, locations covered, products counted, people involved, differences found, likely cause, approved correction, owner of the corrective action, and review date. This turns a count into a management record that can improve the next count.
