A stockout happens when a customer wants an item and the business cannot sell it. The lost sale is only part of the cost. Customers may switch suppliers, staff may spend time searching, and the business loses confidence in its own records.

## Why stockouts happen

Stockouts are often caused by a combination of late purchasing, inaccurate stock records, unrecorded damage, poor visibility across locations, and no agreed reorder process. A product can appear available in a spreadsheet while the shelf is empty, or appear unavailable in one branch while another branch has excess stock.

## Five practical controls

### 1. Set reorder points

Use recent demand, supplier lead time, and a safety buffer to decide when an item needs replenishment. Do not use the same threshold for every product; fast-moving items and long-lead-time items need different treatment.

### 2. Review exceptions every day

Managers do not need to read every transaction. They do need to see products below their reorder level, unusual adjustments, and items selling faster than expected.

### 3. Make receiving a defined step

Count goods against the purchase document before they enter available stock. Record shortages, damage, and substitutions immediately.

### 4. Connect sales to inventory

When a sale is recorded separately from the stock record, the data will always lag behind reality. A connected POS and inventory workflow keeps availability closer to the truth.

### 5. Use transfers before emergency purchases

If one location has excess stock and another is empty, an internal transfer may be faster and cheaper than a new purchase. This requires visibility by location.

## Measure improvement

Track stockout rate, emergency purchases, lost-sale reports, and the accuracy of cycle counts. Review the numbers by product category and location so the team can fix the process causing the issue, not just react to the next empty shelf.

## Classify the products that matter most

Not every item needs the same review frequency. Identify products that generate the most revenue, are essential to customer demand, or take a long time to replace. Count these more often and give their reorder settings more attention. A small number of important items may account for a large share of the service problem.

## Use a simple exception routine

At the start of the day, review items below their reorder point, purchase orders that are late, and products with unusual sales activity. During the day, make it easy for staff to record a customer request for an unavailable product. At the end of the week, compare those requests with stock and purchasing records.

For example, if customers repeatedly ask for a product that is not in the catalogue, the problem is not only replenishment. The business may need to create the item correctly, choose a supplier, and define a minimum quantity before it can be tracked reliably.

## Do not hide stockouts with manual edits

Changing a quantity to make a report look correct removes the evidence needed to fix the process. Record the reason for an adjustment and investigate whether the cause was damage, theft, an incorrect unit conversion, a receiving error, or a transaction that was never completed.

Over time, this discipline turns stockout conversations from “we thought we had it” into a measurable improvement process.
