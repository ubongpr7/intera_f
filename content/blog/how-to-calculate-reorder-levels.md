A reorder level is the point at which a business should start replenishing an item. It is designed to give a new purchase enough time to arrive before available stock reaches zero.

## A simple formula

One practical starting point is:

`Reorder point = average daily demand × supplier lead time + safety stock`

For example, if a product sells an average of 12 units per day, the supplier usually takes 5 days, and the business keeps 20 units as safety stock, the reorder point is 80 units. The formula is a planning aid, not a promise that demand or lead time will never change.

## Use better inputs over time

Review demand by season, promotion, location, and product category. Measure actual supplier lead time rather than relying only on the promised time. A product with unpredictable demand may need a larger buffer, while a slow-moving item may need a smaller purchase quantity.

## Connect the number to a process

A reorder level only helps if someone reviews it and knows what to do next. The workflow should identify the item, suggest or create a purchase, record the receiving process, and update available stock after the delivery is checked.

## Avoid false precision

Reorder levels should be reviewed when prices, suppliers, seasons, or customer demand change. A precise-looking number based on poor data can create more confidence than accuracy. Start with a simple rule, measure the result, and improve it with real transaction history.

## A worked example

Suppose a shop sells an average of 12 units per day. The supplier usually needs 5 days to deliver, and the business keeps 20 units as safety stock. The reorder point is 80 units: 12 multiplied by 5, plus 20. When available stock approaches 80, the buyer should review the next order rather than wait until the shelf is empty.

The buyer should also consider open purchase orders, stock already in transit, and stock held at another location. Ordering 80 more units without checking those quantities can create overstock.

## Reorder point is not order quantity

The reorder point answers “when should we review or start buying?” The order quantity answers “how much should we buy?” Order quantity may depend on supplier minimums, pack sizes, cash available, storage capacity, expected demand, and price breaks. Keep those decisions separate so a large supplier carton does not automatically become a large purchase.

## Review the rule after changes

Revisit reorder levels when a supplier changes lead time, a product becomes seasonal, a promotion changes demand, a branch opens, or prices and cash constraints change. Use actual sales and receiving history when available. A rule that was correct last year can be wrong after the business changes.

## Common mistakes

Avoid using one threshold for every product, counting reserved stock as available, ignoring stock in transit, and treating an average as a guarantee. Reorder levels are decision support; a buyer still needs context and judgement.
