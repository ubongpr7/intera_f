Inventory forecasting is an estimate of what customers are likely to buy and when. It helps a business decide what to purchase, how much cash to commit, and where to position stock. A forecast is useful when it makes uncertainty visible; it is not a promise that demand will follow a single number.

## Identify the demand event

Seasonality may come from festive periods, school calendars, promotions, weather, harvest cycles, pay cycles, or local events. Write down the event and the products it may influence. Do not assume every item rises together; demand may shift between sizes, brands, and price points.

## Use history carefully

Review sales by product, location, and period. Separate normal demand from a one-off promotion, a stockout, a price change, or a supplier delay. If a product was unavailable for two weeks, its sales history understates demand during that period.

## Plan with scenarios

Create a base case, a higher-demand case, and a lower-demand case. Decide what action would trigger an additional purchase, transfer, promotion, or pause. Scenario planning is more useful than pretending the forecast is perfectly accurate.

## Include supplier and cash constraints

A forecast that ignores lead time, minimum order quantities, storage, exchange-rate exposure, or cash availability is incomplete. The purchasing team needs to know not only what might sell but also what can be ordered, received, and funded.

## Review after the event

Compare forecast demand with sales, stockouts, excess stock, emergency purchases, and margin. Record what changed and update the next plan. Over time, the business will build a more useful understanding of its own demand patterns.

## Why forecasting is difficult for small businesses

Many Nigerian business owners are working with changing prices, limited cash, supplier minimums, and customers who switch products when prices move. The goal is not to pretend those conditions are stable. The goal is to make the assumptions visible so the owner can decide what to protect, what to delay, and what to substitute.

For example, a business may forecast higher demand for a festive period but decide not to buy the highest possible quantity because cash is needed for rent, wages, transport, or another product category. A scenario-based plan makes that trade-off explicit instead of leaving it to a last-minute emergency purchase.

## A simple forecast review meeting

Review the previous period’s sales, stockouts, excess stock, supplier performance, price changes, and customer requests. Decide which assumptions changed. Then agree on the next purchase actions, the products to monitor more frequently, and the trigger for changing the plan.

Forecasting becomes more useful when it is connected to actual inventory and purchasing decisions. It should help the business act earlier, not create another report that nobody uses.
