Multi-location inventory is not just one stock list with several addresses. Each location receives, sells, counts, and transfers stock. Without a shared system, the central team may have to call each branch to answer a simple question: where is this product now?

## Create a clear location model

Name every store, warehouse, van, or consignment location consistently. Define whether stock is available for sale, reserved, in transit, damaged, or awaiting receiving. This prevents teams from treating every quantity as immediately sellable.

## Record transfers as two linked movements

A transfer should reduce stock at the sending location and create stock in transit before it is received at the destination. The receiving team should confirm the actual quantity and record shortages or damage. This gives managers a trail instead of a mysterious difference.

## Standardise the count process

Use the same count rules in every branch: when counts happen, who performs them, how differences are approved, and how frequently high-value or fast-moving items are checked. Cycle counts reduce the disruption of trying to count everything at once.

## Use location-level reporting

Look at sales, days of cover, stockouts, dead stock, and adjustments by location. A product that is slow in one branch may be fast in another. Location-level data helps the business transfer stock or adjust purchasing instead of making a blanket decision.

## Give each role a clear responsibility

The central team may own product data and purchasing rules. A branch manager may approve adjustments and review low-stock signals. A storekeeper may receive goods and perform counts. A cashier may sell and process approved returns. Separating these responsibilities makes the workflow easier to train and makes unusual activity easier to review.

## Example: moving a fast seller

Imagine that one branch has 40 units of a product while another branch has none. The team should be able to request a transfer, approve it, record dispatch, show the quantity in transit, and confirm receipt. If the destination receives 38 units, the two-unit difference should remain visible for investigation. Without those states, the business may count the same stock twice or assume it disappeared.

## Metrics worth reviewing

Review stock accuracy by location, transfer time, transfer differences, stockouts, days of cover, dead stock, and adjustments. Look for patterns rather than blaming a branch from a single discrepancy. A location with frequent differences may need better receiving, training, layout, or product labelling.

Multi-location visibility should make the operation more coordinated, not create a central bottleneck. Give local teams the tools and permissions they need, then use shared reporting for exceptions and decisions.

## A rollout sequence that reduces confusion

Start by naming locations and defining what each status means. Then clean the product catalogue and confirm opening balances at one pilot location. Test a transfer, a return, a count, and a correction before adding the next branch. This makes it easier to identify whether a problem comes from the product data, the workflow, or the training.

## Plan for temporary differences

Stock in transit should not be treated as available at both ends. A transfer may be dispatched today and received tomorrow. A clear in-transit state prevents double counting and gives managers a realistic view of what can be promised.

## What good visibility enables

Shared data lets a manager answer practical questions quickly: Can another branch fulfil this request? Which location is overstocked? Which supplier delivery is late? Which branch needs a count? The value is not the dashboard itself; it is the faster, better decision that follows.
