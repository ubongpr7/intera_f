Pharmacies and distributors need accurate inventory because availability, timing, and traceability matter. A product may have different pack sizes, suppliers, batch details, expiry dates, or storage requirements. A record that only shows a total quantity may not answer the operational question.

## Build product data carefully

Use consistent names, strengths, pack sizes, units, supplier references, and selling units. Separate products that look similar but cannot be substituted. Good product data is the foundation for purchasing, selling, and reporting.

## Use receiving as a control point

Compare deliveries with the purchase document. Record shortages, damaged goods, and expiry information before the stock becomes available. This is where a business can prevent downstream confusion and protect the quality of its records.

## Watch movement and ageing

Reports should help the team see fast-moving items, slow-moving items, stock below reorder level, and products approaching an internal review threshold. The business can then adjust purchasing, transfers, or promotions based on evidence.

## Protect accountability

Permissions and an audit trail are important when several staff members can receive goods, sell products, or adjust quantities. The goal is not to make work difficult; it is to make unusual changes visible and explainable.

## A controlled receiving checklist

Before confirming a delivery, check the purchase reference, product identity, quantity, pack size, condition, supplier, batch information where applicable, and expiry information where applicable. Record discrepancies immediately. A later correction is harder because the goods may already have been sold or transferred.

## Make substitutions explicit

When a supplier provides a different pack size, brand, or product, do not quietly change the original line. Record what was ordered and what was received, then decide whether the substitute is acceptable. Clear records protect purchasing analysis and reduce confusion for the next person handling the item.

## Support a repeatable count routine

Count high-value, fast-moving, or sensitive products more frequently. Use a blind count where appropriate so the counter records what is physically present rather than copying the expected number. Review differences with the person responsible for the preceding movement.

## Ask vendors about data ownership

Before implementation, ask how product and transaction data can be exported, how user access is removed, how backups are handled, and how the system records changes. These questions matter for continuity and accountability in every industry, especially when several people handle stock.

## A useful daily review

Review products received, products sold, returns awaiting inspection, items below their reorder level, and adjustments made during the previous day. Look for unusual quantities, repeated corrections, and products that have not moved. The aim is to identify a small number of actions that protect availability and record quality.

## Do not confuse a catalogue with a control system

A long product list does not prove that inventory is controlled. The system also needs disciplined receiving, correct units, location visibility, clear permissions, and a count routine. If those processes are missing, the catalogue will gradually drift away from the physical stock.

## Plan the rollout around risk

Start with the products and workflows where errors are most costly. Train staff on receiving and returns first, because mistakes at those points affect every later report. Then add purchasing analysis, branch transfers, and deeper reporting as the team becomes comfortable with the core record.
