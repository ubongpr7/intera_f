The obvious cost of a stockout is the sale that did not happen. The less visible costs include a customer switching to another supplier, a sales person searching across branches, an emergency purchase at a worse price, and management time spent investigating why the record was wrong.

## Measure the full impact

Start by recording stockout events by product and location. Estimate missed demand from customer requests, sales history, backorders, or a simple lost-sale log. Review whether the cause was late purchasing, inaccurate records, a receiving issue, unexpected demand, or stock held in the wrong location.

## Separate demand problems from process problems

Some stockouts are caused by demand that no forecast could predict. Others are preventable because a reorder point was ignored, a purchase was not received correctly, or an internal transfer was invisible. The response should match the cause.

## Protect customer trust

If an item is not available, staff need a clear alternative: another branch, a substitute product, a backorder, or a realistic expected date. Accurate location visibility makes those conversations more useful.

## Use data to improve purchasing

Track stockout frequency, emergency purchases, supplier lead time, lost-sale estimates, and days of cover. Review the numbers with the team responsible for buying and receiving. A dashboard is useful when it changes a decision, not simply because it contains more charts.

## A simple impact example

Assume a product normally sells 10 units per day at a contribution margin of ₦1,500 per unit. A three-day stockout represents ₦45,000 of direct contribution margin before considering customers who switch, emergency transport, staff time, or the cost of repairing confidence. The exact number will vary, but estimating it helps the team decide which products deserve tighter controls.

## Separate leading and lagging indicators

Stockout rate is a useful lagging indicator: it tells you what already happened. Low-stock alerts reviewed on time, purchase orders received within expected lead time, and cycle-count accuracy are leading indicators: they show whether the process is likely to improve. Review both.

## Use a root-cause review

For each recurring stockout, ask five questions: Was demand recorded? Was the reorder point appropriate? Was the purchase approved and sent? Was the delivery received correctly? Was the stock available at the right location? This prevents the team from treating every incident as a purchasing problem.

Reducing stockouts is not about holding the maximum quantity of everything. It is about making demand, purchasing, receiving, and location decisions visible early enough to act.

## Turn the review into an operating habit

Assign one person to review low-stock and late-purchase exceptions each day. Assign another person to verify that deliveries were received correctly. Managers should review the repeated causes weekly and change a rule, supplier, transfer process, or training step when the same problem appears again.

## Watch for false availability

A stock record can say an item is available even when it is reserved for another order, damaged, held in receiving, or located in a branch that cannot dispatch it quickly. Separate these states so a sales person is not forced to promise stock that cannot realistically reach the customer.

## Five questions for every recurring stockout

1. Was the product and unit recorded correctly?
2. Did the system reflect the last sale or transfer?
3. Was the reorder level appropriate for current demand?
4. Did the supplier deliver on time and in full?
5. Could another location have supplied the customer?
